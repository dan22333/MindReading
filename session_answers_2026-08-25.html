Part 0

Background in plain words — what every piece is, and why we're doing this

No math here. If you already know what an fMRI encoder and a diffusion model are, skip to Part I. Otherwise, read this once and the rest of the guide will click.

The scientific question

The visual cortex is not one uniform sheet — it's carved into regions that each seem to care about a specific kind of thing. One patch lights up for faces, another for places/scenes, another for words, another for bodies. Neuroscientists have spent decades asking, one hand-picked hypothesis at a time, "what does this patch of cortex actually represent?" This project turns that around: instead of guessing a concept and testing it, we ask the brain to show us — synthesize the image that would drive a chosen patch as hard as possible, then read off what it depicts.

The four building blocks

PieceIn one sentenceThe mental picture
VoxelThe smallest unit an fMRI scanner measures — a tiny 3-D cube of brain (~1–2 mm) with one activation number per image you show the subject.A single pixel of a brain scan. Show a photo → each voxel returns "how hard I fired."
ROI (region of interest)A named set of voxels that behave alike — e.g. FFA (faces), PPA (places), VWFA (words).A neighborhood of brain-pixels you've decided to study together. Your "target."
Brain encoder fA model that predicts those voxel numbers directly from an image — image in, predicted brain activation out.A stand-in for the subject in the scanner. Once trained, you can ask "how much would this ROI fire?" for any image, instantly, with no scanner. It has two stages: a frozen vision backbone (CLIP or DINOv2) that turns the image into features, then a small learned read-out that maps features → voxels.
Diffusion model (Stable Diffusion)An image generator that starts from pure noise and denoises it, step by step, into a photorealistic picture.A sculptor who starts with static and, over ~50 passes, carves out a real-looking image. Left alone it makes a plausible image; we want to bias which one.
The whole idea in one sentence. Chain them: let the diffusion model generate an image, ask the brain encoder "how hard does my ROI fire on this?", and push the generator toward images that score higher — repeat until it converges on a picture the ROI loves. Whatever that picture depicts is the region's preferred concept. That push is the "guidance" this whole guide is about.

What "guiding" the generator means

A diffusion model already knows how to nudge noise toward "a realistic image." Guidance adds a second nudge at every denoising step: "…and also toward images my ROI likes." The natural way to compute that second nudge is a gradient — the encoder's answer to "which way should I change the pixels to make the ROI fire more?" You add a little of that direction at each step. This is exactly how the founding paper of this lineage, BrainDiVE, works, and it's the method whose failure mode Part I dissects.

Two words you'll see everywhere

  • Gradient — the direction of steepest increase. "The gradient of ROI-activation with respect to the pixels" = the exact tweak to every pixel that most raises the predicted firing. Guidance = repeatedly take a small step along it.
  • Manifold — the (very thin, curved) set of pixel-arrays that look like real photographs, floating inside the astronomically larger space of all possible pixel-arrays (almost all of which are noise). "On-manifold" = looks like a real image; "off-manifold" = looks like static. Keep these two words in hand — the entire diagnosis in Part I is about a gradient that points off the manifold.

The one-paragraph version of what goes wrong (so Part I has a target)

Here's the tension the rest of the guide unpacks. The generator (Stable Diffusion 2.1) was trained to take direction in one specific "language": CLIP embeddings. If your brain encoder is also built on CLIP, its "make the ROI fire more" gradient is phrased in a language the generator understands, so the push produces a cleaner, real-looking image. But if your encoder is built on DINOv2 (a different vision model that sees the world differently), its gradient is phrased in a language the generator was never taught — so the push, cranked hard enough to matter, drags the image off the manifold into adversarial texture (weird high-frequency static that fools the encoder's score without depicting anything). That's the "prettiness gap." Part I makes this precise; Parts V–VI fix it.

Part I

Why guiding your encoder doesn't work well

Three short steps take you from "how guidance is wired" to a single number that predicts whether your images come out as photos or as static. Read Step 1 slowly — it's the one you asked about, and everything after it is a consequence.

Step 1 — What "gradient guidance" actually computes

Recall the plan from Part 0: at every denoising step, on top of the generator's own "toward a realistic image" nudge, you add a second nudge — "…and toward images my ROI likes." That second nudge is a gradient. This step writes it down and shows that the gradient you actually apply is not quite the one your encoder asked for — it gets passed through a translator first, and that translator is the whole story.

The one thing to take away. Your encoder forms its wish in pixel space ("brighten these pixels, darken those"). But the generator doesn't edit pixels directly — it edits a compressed code called a latent, which a decoder later expands into pixels. So the encoder's pixel-wish has to be translated back through the decoder into a latent-wish before it can be applied. That translation can faithfully carry the wish, or garble it — and which one you get is exactly the CLIP-vs-DINOv2 story.
First, the cast (all you need for eq. 1)
  • z — the latent. A small compressed code (a 4×64×64 tensor for SD, vs. 3×512×512 pixels). The generator works entirely in here; it's cheaper and smoother than raw pixels. This is the thing you're allowed to edit.
  • D — the decoder (the "D" of the VAE). Expands a latent into an actual image: x = D(z). This is the only bridge from the world you edit (latents) to the world your encoder reads (pixels).
  • r — the reward. Your encoder's headline number: the mean predicted ROI activation, r(x) = mean over voxels of f(x). Bigger = the ROI fires harder. This is what you're trying to increase.
  • \(\nabla_x r\) — the encoder's pixel-wish. "If I could touch the pixels directly, this is the exact change that most raises the ROI's firing." Set entirely by your encoder f — CLIP and DINOv2 give different wishes for the same image.
  • \(J_D\) — the decoder's Jacobian (defined below). The translator.
MotivationYour encoder f reads pixels, so its wish \(\nabla_x r\) lives in pixel space. But the only dial you can turn is the latent z. You cannot apply a pixel-wish to a latent directly — they're different spaces. So: what latent change best serves the pixel-wish? That's a chain-rule question — the reward depends on the latent only through the decoder (z → D → x → r).
ApproachWrite the reward as a function of the latent by composing it with the decoder, r(D(z)), and differentiate with respect to z. The chain rule splits the result into two factors — the decoder's local behavior, then the encoder's wish.
\[ g \;=\; \nabla_{z}\, r\big(D(z)\big) \;=\; \underbrace{J_D^{\top}}_{\substack{\text{translator:}\\ \text{decoder Jacobian}}}\; \underbrace{\nabla_{x}\, r(x)}_{\substack{\text{the encoder's}\\ \text{pixel-wish}}}, \qquad J_D=\frac{\partial D}{\partial z} \](1)
OutcomeThe latent update g you actually apply is your encoder's pixel-wish after the decoder's translator \(J_D^{\top}\) has reshaped it. If the pixel-wish is something the decoder can produce by moving the latent, the translation is faithful and you get a coherent image. If it isn't, the translator mangles the wish — and that mangled push is what shows up as texture. Everything in Steps 2–3 is about measuring how badly it gets mangled.
What is the "Jacobian," really — and why a transpose?

Forget the formula for a second. The decoder D is a function: wiggle the latent a little, the output image wiggles some way. The Jacobian \(J_D\) is just the table of "if I nudge this latent number, which pixels move, and how much." It's the decoder's local cause-and-effect chart. Because latent-space and pixel-space have different sizes and shapes, this chart also rotates and stretches any direction you send through it — a clean latent direction can come out as a lopsided pixel direction, and vice-versa.

We want to go the other way — from a pixel-wish back to the latent-nudge that best delivers it. That reverse pass uses the transpose \(J_D^{\top}\) (this is precisely what backpropagation computes; you never build the matrix, autograd applies it for you). The intuition: \(J_D^{\top}\) takes the pixel-wish and answers "given how the decoder actually maps latents to pixels, which latent tweak pushes hardest in the wished-for direction?"

The catch that drives the whole guide: the decoder can only produce pixel-changes that lie in its reachable set — the images it was trained to make (real-looking photos). If your encoder's pixel-wish points at a change the decoder can reach, \(J_D^{\top}\) passes it through cleanly. If the wish points somewhere the decoder can't reach (e.g. "add this precise high-frequency shimmer"), \(J_D^{\top}\) can only return the shadow of it that is reachable — a distorted push. A CLIP encoder tends to wish for reachable things; a DINOv2 encoder often wishes for unreachable ones. Hold that thought — Step 2 makes "reachable vs. not" exact.

Analogy. You (the encoder) want a room repainted a very specific shade. But you can't touch the walls — you can only phone a painter (the decoder) who mixes only from a fixed set of tins. If your shade is mixable from his tins, you get exactly what you asked for. If it isn't, he doesn't refuse — he mixes the closest thing he can, and hands you something a bit off. The decoder never says "no"; it just quietly returns the nearest thing it can make. When your wish is far from anything makeable, "nearest thing" is a mess. \(J_D^{\top}\) is the painter.

Step 2 — Split the wish into a good half and a bad half

Step 1 said the decoder can only carry the part of your wish that it can actually produce. Let's make "the part it can produce" precise. From Part 0: real photos occupy a thin, curved surface — the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) — inside the vast space of all pixel arrays. Any pixel-wish can be split into a piece that slides along that surface (toward a different real photo) and a piece that pokes straight off it (toward noise). Write \(P_{\mathcal M}\) for the operation "keep only the along-the-surface part."

Motivation"Make the ROI fire more" has two completely different kinds of solution, and the encoder's raw wish is a blend of both: (a) turn the photo into a more ROI-preferred photo (a real cat becomes a bigger, more central cat) — useful; or (b) paint a nearly-invisible high-frequency pattern that spikes the encoder's score without changing what the image depicts — an adversarial trick. We need to separate the honest half from the cheating half.
ApproachProject the pixel-wish onto the manifold's tangent plane. The along-surface part is the honest half; the leftover perpendicular part is the cheat.
\[ \nabla_x r \;=\; \underbrace{g_{\parallel}}_{\substack{\text{honest:}\\ \text{a more-ROI-like real photo}}} \;+\; \underbrace{g_{\perp}}_{\substack{\text{cheat:}\\ \text{an adversarial pattern}}}, \qquad g_{\parallel}=P_{\mathcal M}\,\nabla_x r,\quad g_{\perp}=(I-P_{\mathcal M})\,\nabla_x r \](2)
Outcome\(g_{\parallel}\) is exactly the "reachable" part from Step 1 — the decoder carries it faithfully and the image stays a photo. \(g_{\perp}\) is literally an adversarial perturbation (ascending it in raw pixels is textbook how you fool a network). At a gentle guidance strength the diffusion prior scrubs \(g_{\perp}\) away each step. But crank the strength to force real change and \(g_{\perp}\) wins — the image slides off \(\mathcal{M}\) and collapses into texture. That tug-of-war is the failure you saw.

Step 3 — One number that predicts image quality: manifold alignment \(\alpha\)

So the question "will guidance give me a photo or static?" reduces to a single ratio: of your encoder's total wish, what fraction is the honest, along-the-manifold half? Call it \(\alpha\). Near 1 → almost all of the wish is renderable → clean photos. Near 0 → almost all of it is the adversarial cheat → texture.

\[ \alpha(f) \;=\; \frac{\lVert P_{\mathcal M}\,\nabla_x r\rVert}{\lVert \nabla_x r\rVert}\;\in\;[0,1], \qquad \text{CLIP: } \alpha \text{ high} \quad\bullet\quad \text{DINOv2: } \alpha \text{ low} \](3)

Read it as a percentage: \(\alpha=0.9\) means 90% of the push helps and 10% fights you (the prior easily mops that up); \(\alpha=0.3\) means 70% of every step is dragging you toward static, and no amount of tuning the strength escapes that — turning it up scales the good and bad halves together (see below). \(\alpha\) is a property of your encoder's geometry, not of how hard you push.

Why CLIP is aligned (high \(\alpha\)) and DINOv2 is not (low \(\alpha\))

  1. Stable Diffusion's steering wheel is CLIP. The generator was trained to take direction through one specific input — CLIP text embeddings feeding its cross-attention. CLIP is the only semantic language the UNet ever learned to obey. So "CLIP directions" are renderable by construction → they land in the honest \(g_{\parallel}\) half. A CLIP image encoder shares that same geometry (CLIP was trained to line up images and text), so BrainDiVE's gradient is mostly reachable: high \(\alpha\). This is why the original BrainDiVE images look good — not because its encoder is a better brain model.
  2. DINOv2 was trained for a different job — self-supervised patch self-distillation (great for dense correspondence and part structure), never tied to Stable Diffusion's conditioning. Its sharpest "make the ROI fire" directions are ones the generator was never taught to move along → they fall into the off-manifold \(g_{\perp}\) half: low \(\alpha\). Same brain-prediction skill, wrong language for this generator.
  3. Turning up the guidance scale can't rescue it. The scale multiplies the whole wish — it scales \(g_{\parallel}\) and \(g_{\perp}\) by the same factor, so it never improves the ratio \(\alpha\). Past the point where the prior can still absorb \(g_{\perp}\), the cheat half dominates and you get static. (This is why "just push harder" produced the oversaturated/blank collapse, not better images.)
So \(\alpha\) is your entire prettiness gap, cleanly separated from neuroscience accuracy: two encoders can predict voxels equally well yet have wildly different \(\alpha\), and only the low-\(\alpha\) one makes ugly images. You were never fighting a worse brain model — you were fighting a lower \(\alpha\). That reframes the fix: either raise \(\alpha\) (robustify the encoder, or route the gradient through renderable features — Part VI, options 2–3), or make \(\alpha\) irrelevant by never taking the encoder's gradient at all (use it only as a score — Part VI option 1, and Path B in Part IV). Part III surveys every fix; Parts V–VI rank them for your budget.

Part II

The math of gradient guidance, derived slowly

Part I told you what guidance computes and why it can misfire. This part builds the machine from scratch, one motivated step at a time, so eq. (7) — the actual line of code BrainDiVE runs — feels inevitable. The arc: (1) a diffusion model is secretly a gradient of log-probability; (2) so we can peek at the clean image it's heading toward; (3) so we can bend it toward "the ROI fires" with Bayes' rule; (4) which is exactly BrainDiVE; (5–6) plus the two practical fixes that stop it exploding. You can skim this on a first read and come back — Parts III–VI don't require the derivations.

Symbols: \(x_t\) = noisy image at step \(t\); \(\varepsilon_\theta\) = the denoiser's predicted noise; \(\bar\alpha_t\) = retained-signal fraction (\(\approx 1\) early → \(\approx 0\) late); \(V\) = your voxel set; \(s\) = guidance scale.

1 · Diffusion is secretly a "score" model

MotivationTo steer sampling we need the gradient of \(\log p_t(x_t)\) (the "score"). But diffusion models don't output a score — they output predicted noise. Are these the same thing?
ApproachWrite the forward corruption, then read off the identity between predicted noise and the score.
\[ x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon, \qquad \nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)=-\,\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \](4)
OutcomePredicting noise is knowing the score (up to a scale). That's the hook that lets us add an external gradient to the sampler.

2 · The clean-image bridge (Tweedie / predicted-\(x_0\))

MotivationYour encoder only understands clean images, but at step \(t\) you hold a noisy \(x_t\). Feeding \(x_t\) to CLIP/DINO gives a meaningless, adversarial gradient. We need a clean image to score.
ApproachUse the same noise prediction to jump to the model's best clean guess, then score that.
\[ \hat x_0(x_t)=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \](5)
OutcomeOne clean estimate per step, in-distribution for your encoder. This single trick powers DPS, FreeDoM, Universal Guidance, and BrainDiVE alike.

3 · Guidance = Bayes on the score

MotivationYou don't want any image \(p(x)\); you want images where "the ROI fires," i.e. \(p(x\mid y)\). How do you bend the sampler toward the condition \(y\)?
ApproachBayes splits the conditional score into the prior score (the model) plus a reward gradient. Convert to noise form and you get a drop-in correction to \(\varepsilon_\theta\).
\[ \nabla\log p_t(x_t\mid y)=\underbrace{\nabla\log p_t(x_t)}_{\text{the model}}+\underbrace{\nabla\log p_t(y\mid x_t)}_{\text{your reward}}, \qquad \hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\theta-s\,\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(y\mid x_t) \](6)
Outcome"Guidance" is just: run the model, then nudge its noise by your reward gradient. Now we only need to define the reward.

4 · The concrete BrainDiVE step

Model the likelihood as a reward, \(p(y\mid x_t)\propto \exp(r)\), evaluate \(r\) on the clean estimate \(\hat x_0\), and the whole thing is a single backprop through decoder + encoder:

\[ r(x)=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V} w_v\,f_v(x); \qquad g_t=\nabla_{z_t}\, r\big(D(\hat x_0(z_t))\big); \qquad z_t\leftarrow z_t+\lambda_t\,g_t \](7)

5 · The one knob that matters: normalize the step

MotivationRaw \(\lVert g_t\rVert\) swings wildly across timesteps and across ROIs — too small does nothing, too large explodes into texture (this is exactly the blank/oversaturated-image footgun).
ApproachTie the step size to the model's own noise scale so guidance is always commensurate with the denoising step.
\[ \lambda_t \;=\; s\cdot\frac{\lVert\varepsilon_\theta(z_t,t)\rVert}{\lVert g_t\rVert+\delta}, \qquad\text{then clip } \lVert g_t\rVert \](8)
OutcomeAn ROI-independent schedule that stops the collapse — the practical fix that made your images appear at all.

6 · Healing the off-manifold damage

Two cheap add-ons raise quality far more than raising \(s\): (a) time-travel — renoise \(t{-}1\!\to\!t\) and redo the step \(k\) times so the prior erases \(g_{\perp}\); (b) manifold projection — keep only \(P_{\mathcal M} g_t\). Both are detailed in Part III (FreeDoM, MCG/MPGD) and Part V.

Flow-matching caveat. FLUX/SD3 predict a velocity on a straight noise→data path, so \(\hat x_0\) is a linear extrapolation that leaves \(\mathcal M\) faster → gradient guidance drifts harder. Use FLUX as a great generator, but "flow + backprop the encoder" is an open problem (Rectified-CFG++, CFG-MP).

Part IV

A build plan for your paper

Give a voxel set → generate an image → let a vision model name what it sees → edit the image to remove that thing → check if activation drops → iterate, decomposing the concept when it's too coarse. Yes, it makes sense — it's BrainTrace's validated logic, run in the harder, open-ended, voxel-first direction that is your contribution.

The core loop (and its one real measurement)

Part V · Area A

Taming CLIP-shaped gradient-guidance (the fix-stack)

The encoder-agnostic foundation. These make any gradient-guidance cleaner — necessary for CLIP, and they raise the floor for your DINOv2 too, but they can't by themselves fix the manifold mismatch (that's Part VI). CLIP-gradient guidance is a known-finicky method (provably prone to adversarial gradients — Shen et al., NeurIPS 2024), but there is a standard, battle-tested fix-stack from the CLIP-guided-diffusion lineage (Crowson / Disco Diffusion) plus the guidance-theory papers. Below, in priority order (biggest win first).

Root cause, one line. Off-the-shelf CLIP was trained on clean images, so a single full-image CLIP gradient is trivially satisfiable by high-frequency texture that spikes the score without looking like anything. Every fix does one of two things: (a) de-adversarialize the gradient (make it agree across crops/augmentations so only coherent semantic signal survives), or (b) pull the sample back onto the manifold (projection / re-noising / regularizers).

The fix-stack, in priority order

  1. Cutouts — augmentation-averaged CLIP gradient (biggest single win). Crop \(N\) random patches (random size & position), resize each to 224, embed all, average the loss, backprop that. Averaging over crops cancels per-pixel adversarial directions (they don't agree across crops) and keeps only spatially-coherent semantic signal. Typical: \(N=16\) (Disco pushes 30–64), \(\text{cut\_pow}=1.0\), accumulate over 2 batches. Source: nerdyrodent.
    \[ g=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_x\,\ell\big(\mathrm{CLIP}(\mathrm{crop}_i(\hat x_0)),\,\text{target}\big) \](10)
  2. Guide on the clean estimate \(\hat x_0\), never on \(x_t\). Compute the Tweedie predicted-\(x_0\) (eq. 5), feed that to CLIP. CLIP on a noisy \(x_t\) is out-of-distribution → meaningless/adversarial gradient. (BrainDiVE already does this.) Universal across FreeDoM / MPGD / Crowson.
  3. Spherical-distance loss, not raw cosine. Normalize both embeddings and use the squared great-circle distance on the unit sphere — well-behaved near the optimum where cosine flattens. Source: Crowson / nerdyrodent.
    \[ \ell_{\text{sph}}(a,b)=2\Big(\arcsin\tfrac{\lVert\hat a-\hat b\rVert}{2}\Big)^{2}, \qquad \hat a=a/\lVert a\rVert \](11)
  4. Adaptive (noise-norm) step size — the finicky-killer. Scale each guidance step by the model's own score magnitude so guidance nudges rather than overwhelms (also fixes slow convergence). The principled version of eq. 8. Source: Shen et al., NeurIPS 2024.
    \[ \lambda_t=\eta\cdot\frac{\lVert\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert}{\lVert g_t\rVert^{2}} \](12)
  5. Guide only in the "semantic" window; warm up, then turn off. FreeDoM's three stages: chaotic (early — too noisy, skip), semantic (middle — content is decided, guide strongly), refinement (late — guidance only injects texture, reduce/skip). Source: FreeDoM.
  6. Time-travel / resampling. In the semantic window, after stepping \(t\to t{-}1\), re-noise back one step and re-denoise, repeating \(r\approx 2\text{–}10\) times. The prior re-absorbs off-manifold kicks. Source: FreeDoM.
  7. Manifold projection (MPGD / MCG). Project the guidance gradient onto the manifold's tangent space (via the VAE or the model's own \(x_0\)-prediction) so no update leaves the learned image manifold. No training. Source: MPGD.
  8. Cheap on-manifold regularizers. TV loss (penalize adjacent-pixel differences, \(\text{tv}\approx150\)) kills checkerboard texture; range loss \(\lVert x-\mathrm{clamp}(x,-1,1)\rVert^2\) (\(\approx50\)) stops blown-out contrast. Source: nerdyrodent.
  9. Keep Self-Attention Guidance (SAG). Sharpens structure at moderate scale. BrainDiVE uses \(\text{sag}=0.75\). Source: SAG.
Don't just crank the scale. Classifier-guidance theory (Dhariwal & Nichol): too-small = unclear samples, too-large = "patch-boundary artifacts / exaggerated contrast." Sweep, and back off at the first sign of texture.
BrainDiVE's own recipe (verified): SD 2.1-base, 512², empty prompt, 50 steps (DPM-Solver++), SAG 0.75, encoder guidance scale \(\gamma=130\) (repo note: a bug cut effective scale ~10–20%, so use ~100 for OpenCLIP ViT-B, ~150 for EVA02-CLIP), guidance on the predicted-\(x_0\) resized to 224. It ships without cutouts/TV/time-travel — exactly the headroom this fix-stack gives you.

Part VI · Area B

Making guidance work for your DINOv2 universal encoder

This is your problem. Your universal brain-encoder reads voxels off frozen DINOv2 features; SD's UNet only ever learned to obey CLIP-shaped conditioning; so backpropping your DINOv2 objective pushes the latent off-manifold (low \(\alpha\), Part I) → adversarial texture. Below is the full menu, ordered by the axis that matters to you — how much you have to touch the generator — and filtered to your stated budget: light fine-tune OK (small adapter / LoRA), don't retrain the SD backbone. Each card is tagged by how it beats the mismatch: S = score only R = realign gradient C = condition natively G = retrain generator.

The key realization. There are only four ways out of a low-\(\alpha\) encoder: (S) stop using its gradient and use it as a score; (R) make its gradient on-manifold (robustify it, or route through the generator's own features); (C) convert its target into SD's native CLIP conditioning; or (G) retrain the generator to speak DINOv2. Your "light fine-tune, keep SD frozen" budget rules out G as a main plan — so the live options are S, R, and C, shown first.
Fits your budget — keep SD frozen, train at most an adapter / LoRA
1 · Use DINOv2 as a score, not a gradient — RL reward fine-tuning (DDPO / DPOK) — Black et al. 2023; Fan et al., NeurIPS 2023 top pick S
TL;DRNever differentiate the encoder. Let SD learn by trial and reward to make images your voxels like, using your brain-encoder purely as a scalar scorekeeper.
How it works — the logic

A diffusion sampler is a sequence of \(T\) denoising steps. DDPO reframes that sequence as a Markov decision process: the state is the pair \((x_t,t)\), the action is the next latent \(x_{t-1}\) drawn from the policy \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) (= your SD model with trainable LoRA weights \(\theta\)), and the reward is paid only at the end — once, on the finished image \(x_0\):

\[ R(x_0)=a_V(x_0)=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V} f_v(x_0) \quad\text{(your DINOv2 brain-encoder, frozen)} \]

Policy-gradient (REINFORCE / PPO) then nudges \(\theta\) so that trajectories which ended in a high-reward image become more likely. The gradient it actually uses is the score-function estimator — notice the reward appears as a plain multiplicative number, and the only thing differentiated is the model's own log-probability of its actions:

\[ \nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t} \big(R(x_0)-b\big)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\right]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(p_\theta \Vert p_{\text{base}}\big) \]

\(b\) is a baseline (the batch-mean reward) that reduces variance; the \(\mathrm{KL}\) term (or, in practice, PPO's clip) tethers the fine-tuned policy to the frozen base SD so it can't wander off the natural-image manifold to chase reward.

Why it kills the \(\alpha\) mismatch entirely

The whole problem in Part I was that \(\nabla_x f\) points off-manifold. Here \(\nabla_x f\) is never computed — the encoder only ever emits a scalar \(R\). So its gradient geometry, its "adversarial" directions, its low \(\alpha\): all irrelevant. Meanwhile the generator being optimized is the same SD that already knows what real images look like, and the KL leash keeps it there. That's why RL is the one option that makes your exact problem structurally vanish rather than merely dampening it.

Recipe — how to actually do it
  1. Wrap generation to log action probabilities. Sample images with SD 2.1 + current LoRA using a DDIM/ancestral sampler whose per-step transition is a known Gaussian, so \(\log p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is analytic and differentiable in \(\theta\). Store the trajectory and its step log-probs. (Start from ddpo-pytorch or 🤗 TRL's DDPOTrainer and just replace the reward function.)
  2. Score with your encoder. Run each finished \(x_0\) through the frozen DINOv2 brain-encoder → \(R=a_V(x_0)\). This is the only place your encoder is used.
  3. Turn rewards into advantages. Within each batch (ideally per-ROI), standardize: \(A=(R-\text{mean})/\text{std}\). This per-group normalization is the single trick that makes DDPO stable — it removes the need to know the reward's scale.
  4. PPO update on LoRA only. Maximize \(\sum_t A\cdot\log p_\theta\) with the importance ratio clipped to \([1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]\) (lets you take a few gradient epochs per batch of samples). Train a small LoRA (rank 4–16) on the UNet attention projections; SD backbone and your encoder stay frozen.
  5. Loop. sample → score → advantage → update, a few hundred images per round. Track mean reward (should climb) and KL-to-base (should stay bounded).
  6. One LoRA per ROI is the clean design; to scale across ROIs, condition a single LoRA on an ROI-id embedding so they share capacity.
Key settings (sane starting points)
  • LoRA LR \(1\text{–}3\times10^{-4}\); rank 8; batch 64–256 images/round; inner PPO epochs 1–4; clip \(\epsilon\approx10^{-4}\text{–}10^{-2}\) (DDPO runs small).
  • Sampling: 30–50 DDIM steps; CFG can be low or 0 (you're rewarding directly, not prompting).
  • KL weight \(\beta\): tune upward the moment images start looking "off" — it's your realism dial.
Pitfalls & how to dodge them
  • Reward hacking. Your encoder is an imperfect reward; with a weak leash SD will find weird images that spike \(a_V\) without being meaningful. Defenses: stronger KL, lower LR, a small auxiliary realism reward, and a VLM/human spot-check each round.
  • Single-encoder overfit. It may hack your encoder specifically. Hold out a second, independently-trained encoder and require it to also rate the images high — this is your honesty check (and doubles as evidence for the paper).
  • Sample cost. RL burns generations; budget GPU time. DDPO is markedly more sample-efficient than DPOK — prefer it.
You'll know it worked when

Mean \(a_V\) of sampled images rises steadily, KL-to-base stays bounded (images still look natural), and the held-out encoder agrees the images are high-activating. At that point you have an SD that natively mass-produces top-ROI images — cheap to sample from thereafter.

2 · Robustify the encoder — perceptually-aligned gradients — Santurkar 2019; Kawar/Ganz/Elad, TMLR 2022 keeps your exact pipeline R
TL;DRKeep the BrainDiVE gradient pipeline exactly as-is, but first smooth your encoder's input-gradient by adversarial fine-tuning, so \(\nabla_x f\) points at real image edits instead of static.
How it works — the logic

Why is \(\nabla_x f\) adversarial in the first place? Because a standard network has a jagged loss surface in pixel space: a tiny, high-frequency change to the input can swing its features enormously. So the direction of steepest ascent is a high-frequency pattern — off-manifold noise. Adversarial (\(\ell_2\)) training flattens that surface: you force the encoder to give the same answer for every image inside a small ball \(\lVert\delta\rVert_2\le\epsilon\), which forbids it from relying on high-frequency cues. A network that is invariant to small perturbations has, as a mathematical consequence, an input-gradient that is low-frequency and semantically meaningful — the "perceptually-aligned gradients" phenomenon. In our language: robust training raises \(\alpha\).

\[ \min_{w}\ \mathbb{E}_x \max_{\lVert\delta\rVert_2\le\epsilon}\ \ell\big(f_w(x+\delta),\,\text{voxels}(x)\big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \nabla_x f_w \text{ perceptually aligned} \]

You change how the encoder is steered, not what it predicts — with a modest \(\epsilon\), voxel-prediction accuracy is roughly preserved, so your causal test stays trustworthy.

Recipe — how to actually do it
  1. Decide what to robustify (cheapest first). Full DINOv2 adversarial training is expensive; instead put a LoRA on DINOv2's last few blocks + your read-out head and robustify only those. That's enough to reshape the gradient your pipeline sees.
  2. Inner loop — craft the adversary. For each training image \(x\), run PGD: 5–7 steps of \(\delta \leftarrow \Pi_{\lVert\delta\rVert_2\le\epsilon}\big(\delta + \tfrac{\epsilon}{4}\,\tfrac{\nabla_\delta \ell}{\lVert\nabla_\delta \ell\rVert}\big)\), maximizing your voxel-regression loss.
  3. Outer loop — train for invariance. Update \(w\) to minimize the loss on the perturbed \(x+\delta\), using your normal NSD image→voxel objective. Repeat over the dataset.
  4. (Cheaper surrogate.) If PGD is too slow, add a gradient-norm / Jacobian penalty \(\lambda\lVert\nabla_x f_w\rVert^2\) to the ordinary training loss — a first-order approximation that also smooths gradients, at ~2× cost.
  5. Re-run BrainDiVE guidance with the robustified encoder, plus the Part V hygiene (cutouts, adaptive step, time-travel).
Key settings
  • \(\epsilon\) is the master knob: \(\ell_2\ \epsilon\approx 0.5\text{–}3\) on normalized pixels. Larger \(\epsilon\) → more perceptually-aligned gradients but more accuracy loss. Sweep it.
  • PGD steps 5–7, step size \(\epsilon/4\); small encoder LR (it's a fine-tune, not from scratch).
Pitfalls & flags
  • Accuracy/robustness trade-off. Too-large \(\epsilon\) tanks held-out voxel accuracy — and then your \(\Delta_c\) causal test is measuring a worse encoder. Watch clean accuracy every sweep point.
  • Research flag. The two halves — "robust classifier ⇒ clean guidance" and "robust DINO ⇒ better perceptual metric" — are each published, but nobody has published the exact composition (robust DINOv2 → diffusion activation-max). Treat this as a well-motivated bet and verify visually.
You'll know it worked when

Three checks: (a) held-out voxel-prediction accuracy is roughly unchanged; (b) if you visualize \(\nabla_x a_V\) on a real photo, it now looks like a coherent edit (add/remove a recognizable thing) rather than TV static; (c) BrainDiVE-guided images become coherent.

3 · Readout-head guidance — turn an off-manifold external gradient into an on-manifold internal one — Luo et al., CVPR 2024 (Highlight) R
TL;DRKeep gradient guidance, but change what you differentiate: teach a tiny head to read your voxel target off SD's own internal activations, then guide through that head. The gradient is on-manifold by construction.
How it works — the logic

The mismatch exists because the gradient comes from an external encoder whose feature space SD doesn't share. Flip the source. The diffusion UNet's own hidden activations \(\phi_t(x_t)\) are — by definition — features the generator can move along: nudging the latent to change \(\phi_t\) is exactly the kind of change SD renders cleanly. So if a small head \(h_\psi\) can predict your voxel target from those internal features, then guiding by \(\nabla h_\psi\) sends the latent along on-manifold directions. You've separated concerns: DINOv2 supplies the labels (what to look for) offline; the UNet's own representation supplies the on-manifold carrier at sampling time.

\[ \text{train}\ \ h_\psi\big(\phi_t(x_t),\,t\big)\approx a_V(x); \qquad\text{sample-time guidance}\ \ g_t=\nabla_{z_t}\,h_\psi\big(\phi_t(z_t),t\big) \]
Recipe — how to actually do it
  1. Build a labeled set. Take a large image set and run your frozen DINOv2 brain-encoder to get \(a_V\) labels for each (or use real NSD activations). These labels are the only place DINOv2 enters.
  2. Harvest UNet features across time. For each image, run SD's forward (noising) process to many timesteps \(t\) and capture chosen intermediate activations \(\phi_t\) — the mid-block and a couple of up-block layers are the standard picks (Readout Guidance uses exactly this).
  3. Train the heads. Fit small conv/MLP heads \(h_\psi(\phi_t,t)\to a_V\) with an MSE loss, UNet frozen. Train across the full range of \(t\) so guidance has signal at every denoising step (a per-timestep or time-conditioned head).
  4. Guide at sampling. At each step, compute \(h_\psi(\phi_t(z_t),t)\), backprop to \(z_t\), and add \(\lambda_t\,\nabla_{z_t}h_\psi\) using the normalized step (eq. 8). Because the gradient flows only through UNet features, it stays on-manifold — combine with Part V hygiene for extra polish.
Key settings
  • Which layers: start with the UNet mid-block; add an up-block if the head under-fits.
  • Head: 2–3 layer MLP/conv, time-embedding-conditioned; cheap to train.
  • Guidance scale needs its own sweep — but it's far tamer than raw DINO because the direction is already renderable.
Pitfalls
  • Head fidelity is the ceiling. If \(h_\psi\) can't predict \(a_V\) well from UNet features, guidance is weak. Check its held-out \(R^2\) before trusting the guided images.
  • Timestep coverage. A head trained only at low-noise \(t\) gives no signal early in sampling — train across \(t\).
You'll know it worked when

The head predicts held-out \(a_V\) with decent \(R^2\), and guiding with it raises measured \(a_V\) while keeping images coherent — with none of the texture blow-ups the raw DINOv2 gradient produced.

4 · Condition natively — DINOv2→CLIP bridge + IP-Adapter / unCLIP — Ye et al. 2023 (IP-Adapter) C
TL;DRStop pushing a gradient through SD's back door. Translate your DINO target into CLIP language and hand it to SD's normal conditioning input — the door it was built to listen through.
How it works — the logic

SD only "hears" through CLIP-shaped conditioning (that's the whole reason for the mismatch). Gradient guidance fights this by shoving a signal in from outside; conditioning instead uses the front door. Two facts make it possible: (1) SD variants like IP-Adapter (a decoupled cross-attention branch for an image embedding) and Stable-unCLIP accept a CLIP image embedding as conditioning; (2) a small learned map can translate a DINOv2 embedding into that CLIP-image space. Chain them and SD receives your target in the exact format it was trained on — so there is no inference-time gradient at all, nothing that can go adversarial.

\[ \hat e_{\text{DINO}}=\arg\max_{e}\ (\text{read-out} \circ e)\ \ \text{on the DINO sphere};\quad c=B(\hat e_{\text{DINO}})\in\text{CLIP-space};\quad x\sim p_\theta(x\mid c) \]
Recipe — how to actually do it
  1. Get a DINO target for the ROI. Either optimize an embedding \(\hat e_{\text{DINO}}\) to maximize your voxel read-out (a cheap optimization in embedding space — its "adversarialness" is harmless because you never render directly from it), or simply average the DINO embeddings of the top real activating images.
  2. Learn the bridge \(B\): DINO→CLIP-image. On a large image set, collect pairs \(\big(\text{DINO}(x),\ \text{CLIP}_{\text{img}}(x)\big)\) and fit a small MLP with a cosine + MSE loss. This is your one "light fine-tune."
  3. Map and condition. Compute \(c=B(\hat e_{\text{DINO}})\) and feed it to an off-the-shelf IP-Adapter (image-prompt) or Stable-unCLIP. Optionally add a text prompt for global scene layout.
  4. Generate normally — standard CFG, no guidance gradient.
Key settings
  • Bridge: 2–4 layer MLP; loss = cosine(1−sim) + \(\lambda\)·MSE; the more paired data the better the map.
  • IP-Adapter conditioning scale: sweep — higher = more faithful to the target, lower = more natural.
Pitfalls & flags
  • The map is lossy. CLIP and DINO don't encode identical information, so \(B\) discards whatever CLIP can't represent — your target gets blurred. Expect a quality ceiling below the RL/robust routes.
  • Unproven parts. The community DINO→CLIP bridges are non-peer-reviewed, and IP-Adapter's own (reportedly better) DINO variant was never released. No primary source shows this end-to-end at high fidelity — use it as a fast baseline / sanity check, not the centerpiece.
You'll know it worked when

Images generated by conditioning on \(B(\hat e_{\text{DINO}})\) score high under your encoder and look natural — and you can put that number head-to-head against the gradient route in the paper's comparison.
